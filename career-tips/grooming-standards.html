Cabin Crew Grooming and Presentation Standards

Published in advance, assessed on the day, and entirely learnable. What the standards cover, why they exist, and how to meet them exactly.

Interview Tips 8 min read Updated 13 August 2026

Grooming standards are the part of cabin crew recruitment that candidates worry about most and understand least. They are not arbitrary, they are not a beauty contest, and they are published in advance — which makes them one of the few parts of the process you can get completely right before you walk in.

Why the standards exist

Three reasons, none of them about appearance for its own sake. The uniform represents the airline, and consistency across thousands of crew is part of what the brand is. A tidy, controlled presentation signals to passengers that the crew is in control — which matters enormously on the day something goes wrong. And practically, hair off the face, secured jewellery and safe footwear are operational requirements in a cabin where you may need to move fast.

What the standard really tests at an assessment day is whether you can meet a published specification precisely, on a day that matters. That is exactly what the job asks of you every time you report for a flight.

Read the airline's own standard first

Requirements differ meaningfully between airlines, and they change. Do not rely on a video from three years ago, a forum post, or another airline's rules. Find the current published guidance for the airline you are applying to and follow that. Where an airline says nothing on a point, conservative and neutral is the safe reading.

The general principles

  • Hair. Clean, tidy, off the face, and in a style you could keep in place through a fourteen-hour duty. Long hair is usually required to be up. Natural or naturally plausible colours are the norm; strong fashion colours are commonly excluded.
  • Make-up. If worn, neutral and well applied rather than heavy. Some airlines specify make-up for certain roles and some do not require it at all — check.
  • Nails. Short, clean and even, whether or not you wear polish. Chipped polish reads worse than none.
  • Jewellery. Minimal. Typically a watch, a plain ring, and small studs. Anything that can catch, swing or be lost in a cabin is a problem.
  • Fragrance. Very light or none. Cabins are small, shared, and full of people who may already feel unwell.
  • Shoes. Polished, closed, comfortable enough to stand in for a full day.
  • Clothing for an assessment day. Business dress in conservative colours, well fitted and pressed. You are dressing as though you already work there.

Tattoos, piercings and visible marks

Policies here vary more than any other area, and they have been changing. Some airlines prohibit any visible tattoo in uniform, some allow small ones in defined areas, and a growing number have relaxed their rules. The same applies to piercings beyond a single ear stud. Because the variation is so wide, this is precisely the point to check the airline's current published policy rather than assume — and if the wording is ambiguous, asking the recruitment team directly is entirely reasonable and reflects well on you.

Scars, skin conditions and other visible marks are generally assessed against whether they can be presented neatly in uniform, not against appearance. If you have a specific concern, ask rather than rule yourself out on a guess.

Height, reach and fitness

Strictly these are requirements rather than grooming, but they are usually checked on the same day. Most airlines specify a reach measurement — the height you can touch flat-footed or on tiptoe — because it determines whether you can access overhead safety equipment. Some also state a height range or a height-to-weight guideline. These are published; measure yourself honestly at home first.

Build a routine you can repeat at four in the morning

The candidates who look composed at an assessment day are almost never the ones improvising. They have practised the hair, they know how long it takes, and they have a small kit that fixes the three things most likely to go wrong. Once hired, that same routine has to survive early reports, time zones and hotel bathrooms, so it is worth building something simple and repeatable rather than elaborate.

What this is not

It is not a judgement of how you look. Airlines hire a very wide range of people, of every appearance, from every background, and they always have. The standard is about consistency and control, and every part of it is learnable. Nobody has ever been hired for a hairstyle — but plenty of strong candidates have lost a day because they did not read a page that was published for them in advance.

The short version

  • The standard tests whether you can follow a published specification precisely.
  • Always use the airline's current guidance, not another airline's or an old video.
  • Tattoo and piercing policies vary widely and have been changing — check, don't assume.
  • Build a routine simple enough to repeat before an early report.
  • Nobody is hired for a hairstyle, but candidates do lose days by not reading the page.
