What Happens After Your Cabin Crew Interview

The waiting, the talent pool, the checks that follow an offer — and the one request that should always stop you cold.

Interview Tips 9 min read Updated 13 August 2026

The interview ends, you walk out, and then nothing happens for what feels like a very long time. This stage is where most candidates lose their nerve — not because anything has gone wrong, but because nobody explained what the silence means. Here is what is usually happening on the other side of it.

How long the wait really is

It varies enormously. Some airlines tell you the same day. Some take two weeks. Six to eight weeks is entirely normal for a large intake, and longer is not unusual where an airline is recruiting across several bases at once. The airline is typically working through a large number of candidates from multiple assessment days, coordinating with training slots that may be months out.

A long silence is not a rejection, and a fast response is not always an acceptance. Try not to read anything into the timing at all.

What the outcomes actually are

  • Progression to a further stage. A final panel, a language assessment, or an online test.
  • A conditional offer. Subject to medical, document and background checks — the most common form a first offer takes.
  • The talent pool. Also called a hold pool. You met the standard but there is no immediate seat. This is a genuine outcome, not a polite refusal, and people are called from these pools regularly — sometimes months later.
  • Unsuccessful. Usually with no individual feedback, which is frustrating but standard at this volume.

The checks that follow an offer

A conditional offer starts a process that is more thorough than most jobs, because the role sits inside an aviation security and safety regime:

  • Medical assessment. Vision, hearing, general fitness, and often a swim test — many airlines require you to swim a set distance and tread water unaided.
  • Background and reference checks. Frequently a full employment history covering the last five years with no unexplained gaps, plus criminal record checks for airside access.
  • Right to work and visas. Where the base is abroad, this can be the longest single step.
  • Document verification. Certificates, passport validity, and sometimes proof of address history.

Answer these promptly and completely. Slow or inconsistent paperwork is one of the few things that can genuinely cost you a place at this stage.

Training and bonding

An offer normally comes with a training course date. Initial training is intensive, several weeks long, safety-focused, and examined — you can be released for failing it. Many airlines also apply a training bond: if you leave within a defined period, a portion of the training cost is repayable. This is legitimate and disclosed in advance. Read the figure, the period and the repayment terms before you sign, and ask if anything is unclear.

What to do while you wait

  • Keep applying elsewhere. Nothing is real until a contract is signed. Candidates who stop after one promising interview lose months.
  • Keep your documents current. Passport validity in particular.
  • Do not chase weekly. One polite follow-up after the stated timeframe has passed is reasonable. More than that reads badly.
  • Watch your email properly. Check spam and promotions folders. Offers do get missed this way, and some have short acceptance windows.
  • Prepare as though you got it. Swim practice, savings for relocation, and an honest look at whether the base suits your life.

If the answer is no

Most airlines apply a reapplication waiting period — commonly six or twelve months — and it is usually stated in the rejection email. Use it rather than resent it. Identify the stage you reached, work on that specific thing, and apply again with something demonstrably different: more customer-facing experience, a stronger CV, a language, a better-prepared set of answers. Reapplying successfully is extremely common.

One warning worth repeating

No legitimate airline asks a candidate for money after an interview. Not for a training deposit, not for a visa "processing fee", not for a uniform, not to secure a place on a course. If a message asking for payment arrives — even one that looks convincing and uses the airline's branding — stop, do not pay, and verify directly with the airline through contact details you find yourself rather than any in the message. This is the stage where recruitment scams are most often successful, because the candidate is hopeful and the request arrives at exactly the moment it would make sense.

The short version

  • Six to eight weeks of silence is normal and means nothing on its own.
  • A talent pool place is a real outcome, not a polite rejection.
  • Offers are conditional on medical, background and document checks — answer them fast.
  • Keep applying elsewhere until a contract is signed.
  • No legitimate airline asks a candidate for money. Verify independently, never pay.
